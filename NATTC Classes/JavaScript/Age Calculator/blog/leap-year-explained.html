Leap Year Explained Simply

Every four years, our calendar gains an extra day — February 29th. But why does this happen, and what would occur if we didn't add it? The answer lies in the mismatch between human timekeeping and the motion of our planet.

The Astronomy Behind It

Earth takes approximately 365.2422 days to orbit the Sun — not a clean 365. That extra 0.2422 of a day accumulates over time. After four years, it adds up to roughly 0.9688 days — close enough to one full day that we insert February 29 to compensate.

Without this correction, our calendar would drift relative to the seasons by about 6 hours per year. After 100 years, the calendar would be off by 24 days. Summer would start in what we call July, not June.

The Three Rules of Leap Years

The Gregorian calendar (used worldwide today) uses a refined three-rule system:

  • A year is a leap year if it is divisible by 4…
  • …except for years divisible by 100, which are not leap years…
  • …unless the year is also divisible by 400, in which case it is a leap year.

Examples: 2024 ✓ (÷4), 1900 ✗ (÷100 but not ÷400), 2000 ✓ (÷400).

Quick Test: Is Your Year a Leap Year?

Formula: (year % 4 === 0 && year % 100 !== 0) || year % 400 === 0

Try it: Is 2028 a leap year? 2028 ÷ 4 = 507, not divisible by 100 → Yes, leap year.
Is 2100? 2100 ÷ 4 = 525, but 2100 ÷ 100 = 21 and 2100 ÷ 400 ≠ integer → No, not a leap year.

Leap Day Birthdays

People born on February 29 — called "leaplings" — only have a technically correct birthday once every four years. Legally, most countries treat February 28 or March 1 as their official birthday in non-leap years. AgeVault detects leap-day birthdays and highlights this fact in your results.

Historical Context

Julius Caesar introduced the first version of the leap year in 46 BC with the Julian calendar — every 4 years, no exceptions. Pope Gregory XIII refined this in 1582 with the century rule, creating the Gregorian calendar we still use today. The correction was accurate enough that we won't need another adjustment for about 3,000 years.
