How to Calculate Age Accurately

Age calculation sounds simple — subtract the birth year from today's year. But the truly accurate method is more nuanced, and getting it right matters for legal documents, medical records, and official applications.

The Simple Method (and Why It Falls Short)

The most common approach is: Current Year − Birth Year = Age. If you were born in 1990 and it's 2026, that gives 36. But this ignores whether your birthday has actually passed this year. If today is March 1st and your birthday is December 15th, you haven't turned 36 yet — you're still 35.

The Correct Method: Calendar Arithmetic

Accurate age calculation requires three passes:

  • Years: Start with current year minus birth year.
  • Months: If the current month is before the birth month, subtract 1 from years and adjust months.
  • Days: If the current day is before the birth day, subtract 1 from months and add the number of days in the previous month.

This is exactly the method used by AgeVault, and it matches how age is formally recognized in most legal jurisdictions.

Why Leap Years Matter

Leap years add an extra day — February 29. When calculating total days lived, you must count whether each year in the span was a leap year. Ignoring this introduces an error of roughly 1 day per 4 years of life. For a 40-year-old, that's about 10 uncounted days.

Month Length Variation

Months have 28, 29, 30, or 31 days. When a partial month is at the end of an age calculation, the "remaining days" must be measured against the actual number of days in that month — not a fixed 30-day assumption. AgeVault handles this by checking the real calendar month length at runtime.

Edge Cases to Watch For

  • Born on February 29 (leap day) — only occurs every 4 years
  • Calculating age across a year boundary (e.g., born December, calculated in January)
  • Time zone differences when calculating age at a precise birth time
  • Gregorian vs Julian calendar for very old historical dates
